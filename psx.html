CASE STUDY · EQUITY ANALYTICS

Where should the money go across Pakistan's thirty most traded stocks?

The KSE-30 is Pakistan's thirty most traded companies. I pulled three years of daily prices, worked out the risk and return of each one, and solved for the split that earns the most for the risk taken. The surprise was what decided a good holding. Every stock rose about half the time. What paid was how far it rose when it did, not how often.

The question

If you were allocating across the KSE-30, Pakistan's thirty most liquid listed companies, how should the money be split, and what actually drives the difference between a good holding and a bad one?

Answering that needed three things that did not exist together anywhere: current prices, a way to turn three years of daily returns into an allocation, and a view of what was driving the result. I built all three.

1. Getting the data

KSE-30 pricing is not available as a clean feed, so I wrote a Python scraper to pull the latest prices for every constituent from Investing.com. That removed the manual collection step entirely and meant the analysis could be refreshed rather than rebuilt.

2. Turning returns into an allocation

In Excel I worked through three years of daily returns to compute per stock risk and return, then built the portfolio risk and return from them.

The allocation itself came from Excel Solver, maximising the portfolio Sharpe ratio subject to the weights summing to 100%. The output is not a chart, it is a decision: the percentage of capital to put into each name.

3. Finding out what drives it

The Power BI report is where the analysis stops describing and starts explaining. Thirteen visuals, filterable by year, month and industry, built on measures for Sharpe ratio, risk, average return, win rate, average gain and average loss.

The allocation Solver produced

Maximising portfolio Sharpe subject to the weights summing to 100% returned a deliberately concentrated book. Two names take 58% of the capital and the top three take 68%, while one is effectively dropped.

The biggest holding is not the best performer

FFC takes the largest allocation at 30% while ranking only fourth on daily expected return. SAZEW has double FFC's return and gets less weight. PAEL matches ATRL's return exactly and is allocated almost nothing.

That is the optimiser doing its job. Sharpe maximisation prices in volatility and how each holding moves against the others, so a steadier mid return name earns weight that a higher return, higher variance name does not. Ranking by return alone would have produced a different and worse book.

Two tools, same answer

The Excel optimiser and the Power BI report were built separately and agree. Solver put its two largest weights in FFC and SAZEW. The dashboard's Sharpe ratio table independently ranks Sazgar Engineering at 1.03 and Fauji Fertilizer at 0.94 as the two best risk adjusted performers in the index. Arriving at the same conclusion by two routes is the closest thing to a check this analysis has.

WHAT THESE NUMBERS ARE NOT

The optimised portfolio reports an annualised return of 84%, risk of 26% and a Sharpe of 3.28. Those are in sample figures. Solver was asked to find the weights that maximise Sharpe on exactly the three years of returns used to measure it, so the result is fitted to that window and will not repeat out of sample. A Sharpe above 3 is not a realistic forward expectation for a long only equity book. The allocation is a defensible starting point and the method is sound; the performance figures are an upper bound on a known period, not a forecast.

Win rate is not the edge

This is the finding worth leading with. The success ratio table puts win rate, average gain and average loss side by side for every company, and the win rates sit in a tight band around 47%. Pakistan Oilfields at 0.51 is barely distinguishable from Habib Bank at 0.46.

The Key Influencers visual confirms what that implies: of industry, company, win rate, average loss and average gain, it is average gain that moves the Sharpe ratio most. Frequency of being right was close to constant across the index. Magnitude was not, and magnitude is where the risk adjusted return came from.

Stock selection dominates

Sazgar Engineering returned a Sharpe of 1.03 against 0.31 for the universe as a whole, with Fauji Fertilizer at 0.94 and National Bank at 0.79 behind it. The spread between the best and the average is wide enough that which names you hold matters more than marginal changes to how much of each.

There is a seasonal pattern

Autumn carried the strongest average return of any season by a clear margin, with winter the weakest. Year on year, 2024 stood out sharply while 2022 and 2023 were both negative, so the three year average flatters a period that was really one strong year carrying two poor ones.

What I would do next

  • Test whether the autumn effect survives out of sample, or whether it is an artefact of 2024 being the one strong year in the window.
  • Re-run the Solver allocation on a rolling window and test it forward on held out data, which is the only way to know how much of the 3.28 Sharpe is signal and how much is fitting.
  • Add a transaction cost assumption. With average gains and losses both around 2%, costs are large enough relative to the edge to change the ranking.
