CASE STUDY · RIDE HAILING

The client thought they needed more drivers.

They didn't. Demand did outstrip supply, but drivers were sitting idle 72.3% of the time while requests went unanswered. Hiring more drivers would have cost money and changed nothing. The constraint was not how many drivers were online. It was whether taking the ride was worth it.

The brief, and where it was wrong

Jeeny, a Middle Eastern ride hailing platform, came in with a hypothesis already formed: too many ride requests were going unfulfilled because there were not enough drivers on the road. The ask was to confirm it and size the shortfall.

The first pass supported them. Requests consistently ran ahead of completed rides. On that evidence alone the answer would have been to recruit, and the project would have ended there.

WHAT THE CLIENT BELIEVED

Unfulfilled demand is a supply shortage. Not enough drivers are online to take the rides being requested.

WHAT THE DATA SHOWED

Supply was short, but drivers were also idle 72.3% of the time. They were online and declining. The binding constraint was acceptance, not headcount.

Those two facts cannot both be explained by a shortage. If drivers were scarce and demand was high, idle time should have been low. It was not. That contradiction is the whole finding: supply was not being converted into rides, and adding more supply would have inherited the same conversion problem.

The funnel made the leak visible: 752K price checks became 578K ride requests and only 203K boarded rides. Roughly two in three people who asked for a ride never took one.

Breaking fulfilment down by hour showed it was not uniform. The rate collapses to about 15% in the small hours and recovers through the morning, which is the signature of a pricing and incentive problem tracking the clock, not a fleet that vanishes overnight.

Pinpointing when it hurts

A total is not actionable. Operations needs to know which shift to fix first, so the last view maps average unfulfilled requests across all 168 day and hour combinations and flags the worst one automatically.

The answer: Sunday at 07:00, where roughly 396 requests an hour go unanswered. That is a specific shift a pricing rule can target, rather than a platform wide intervention.

That view is not a stock Power BI visual. It is a Python visual running inside the report, so the annotation finds the peak in the data rather than being typed in by hand and going stale the moment the filters change.

The recommendation

Because the problem was acceptance rather than headcount, recruitment was the wrong lever. Two changes were proposed, both aimed at making the marginal ride worth taking at the moment it is offered.

  • Demand based dynamic pricing. Fares that move with demand level and time of day, so that the hours with the worst fulfilment carry the strongest incentive to accept. The heat map gives the exact windows to price up.
  • A fare negotiation option. Let riders and drivers settle on a price rather than forcing a take it or leave it fare, which converts some of the requests that currently die at the decline into completed rides.

Both are cheaper than recruiting, and both can be tested against the same fulfilment rate the dashboard already tracks, so the fix is measurable in the tool that found the problem.

A single Deep Dive page carrying eighteen visuals: KPI cards, a conversion funnel, daily demand against completed rides, requests versus completions by weekday, and fulfilment rate by hour. Month and weekday slicers drive the whole page.

What I would do next

  • Split idle time by driver cohort to see whether declines concentrate in a minority of drivers or are spread across the fleet. The fix differs sharply depending on which.
  • Model the fare elasticity needed to lift acceptance in the worst windows, so the pricing change ships with a target rather than a direction.
  • Track fulfilment rate against the same hour and weekday grid after rollout, using the existing report as the control.
